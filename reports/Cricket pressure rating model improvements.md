# Rebuild Pressure Lab around calibrated surfaces and pooled skill

Pressure Lab's biggest gains will come from changing how it handles three things it already models: the chase end-game, the leverage scale and the pressure delta. Adding exotic algorithms matters much less. The most important external finding is a 2026 preprint on IPL chases. It shows that an exact, independence-assuming dynamic-programming (DP) chase model is badly miscalibrated on 2024–26 data (log loss 0.801, worse than a constant forecast), while a one-feature required-run-rate logistic scores 0.438 ([Mishra, arXiv 2608.14696](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.14696)). That bears directly on Pressure Lab's last-12-balls DP. The second finding comes from baseball, the NFL, the NBA and tennis: clutch residuals (pressure performance minus a player's own baseline) barely repeat from one year to the next. Baseball's best estimate needs about 79% regression to the mean even after roughly 325 clutch plate appearances ([Tango](https://tangotiger.net/clutch.html)). Pressure Lab's own null result, that past pressure experience does not predict later pressure performance, is therefore expected rather than a bug. The right response is a model in which a "pressure rating" is overall skill applied in high-leverage states plus a small, heavily pooled clutch slope. In practice: compute leverage from one consistent surface and normalise it so an average ball scores 1.0; replace the two-stage empirical-Bayes pipeline with a crossed batter–bowler mixed model that has pressure random slopes; switch to a single multinomial ball-outcome model; and validate everything out of time. Several of the closest cricket analogues are 2026 preprints or blogs, so some recommendations rest on unreviewed work. The final section flags each one.

## The chase end-game DP is the model's weakest link

Published in-play win-probability (WP) models fall into three families. The first is DP or Markov models over balls, wickets and runs or target, such as WASP, which has been on TV since 2012 ([Wikipedia: WASP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WASP_(cricket_calculation_tool))). The second is direct classifiers, such as Asif & McHale's dynamic logistic regression, whose coefficients evolve smoothly through the match ([IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/intfor/v32y2016i1p34-43.html)). The third is player-level ball simulators from the Swartz group ([SFU PDF](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/t20sim.pdf)). Industry tools such as CricViz WinViz and the ESPNcricinfo Forecaster publish no accuracy or calibration figures, so they offer design ideas but no benchmarks ([Swarajya/CricViz](https://swarajyamag.com/sports/swarajya-speaks-to-cricviz-the-team-behind-the-data-intelligence-models-for-cricket-world-cup-2019); [AIM on IITM-ESPNcricinfo](https://analyticsindiamag.com/iit-madras-espncricinfos-ai-powered-tool-is-enhancing-the-ipl-experience-this-season/)).

The closest analogue to Pressure Lab is Mishra (2026). It uses 1,162 IPL chases with a 2024–26 test set of 164 matches. Held-out Brier / log loss / ECE scores are as follows: **exact base Markov 0.204 / 0.801 / 0.224; RRR-conditioned Markov plus era adjustment 0.149 / 0.474 / 0.131; logistic with engineered features 0.135 / 0.416 / 0.070** ([arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.14696)). The exact model's per-ball outcome marginals match the data to within a total-variation distance of 0.02. Its error comes instead from **run-scoring persistence over roughly 3–5 balls**, a hidden-regime dependence that per-ball independence cannot represent. As a result the model is over-dispersed, overconfident in easy chases and too pessimistic at RRR 10–12 (0.183 predicted vs 0.294 observed) ([arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.14696)). Adding the striker's balls faced to the state barely helped. A 20-ball block bootstrap closed only about 26% of the gap ([arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.14696)).

The paper's central claim is a structural trade-off: **"exactly attributable leverage and well-calibrated WP cannot be obtained from the same object"** ([arXiv abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.14696)). Pressure Lab currently blends both kinds of object, an ML surface early and the exact DP late. Its cleanest fix is therefore a two-surface design. A calibrated ML surface, with isotonic calibration applied to the *final blended* output, would supply displayed WP. A single RRR- and era-conditioned Markov surface would supply leverage and WPA, with its over-dispersion documented rather than recalibrated away. Mishra rejects post-hoc recalibration of the attribution surface because it breaks the martingale property ([arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.14696)). Asif & McHale's smoothly varying coefficients also suggest replacing the fixed linear DP-to-ML ramp over balls 13–24 with a blend weight fitted as a smooth function of balls left. Mishra's benchmark of a Brier score of about 0.135–0.14 on IPL 2024–26 chases gives Pressure Lab a directly comparable target. Its AUC of about 0.79 says nothing about calibration.

The first-innings drift of about 10 points in 2025–26 has a clear cause. IPL scoring has kept rising since the 2023 Impact Player rule: the first-innings average went from 168.2 (2018–22) to 184.7 ([ESPNcricinfo](https://www.espncricinfo.com/ci/content/story/1436641.html)), and 200+ totals went from 37 in 2023 to 41 in 2024 to 52 in 2025 ([ESPNcricinfo](https://africa.espn.com/cricket/story/_/id/48474161/ipl-2026-run-fest-already-flying-fifties-sixy-powerplays-routine-200s)). A single binary era flag cannot follow a drift that keeps going. WASP and WinViz both anchor the first innings to a par score that is re-set from recent venue data or expert judgement ([NZC](https://www.nzc.nz/news-items/archive/whats-wasp-all-about/)). Brooker & Hogan treat match conditions as a nuisance parameter that is re-estimated for each match ([IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/p/cbt/econwp/11-44.html)). Three plug-ins follow. First, a rolling scoring-environment covariate, such as an exponentially weighted league or venue run rate known before the match. Second, isotonic recalibration on a rolling, innings-specific window. Third, a design inference that no source has validated: compose first-innings WP as Σ_T P(final total = T | state) × (1 − WP₂(target T+1)). The first innings would then inherit the calibration of the chase surface, and a level shift in scoring would partly cancel out instead of being baked into a classifier trained on data up to 2024.

## Leverage should be a ratio to the average ball, not a log of a capped swing

Pressure Lab's "expected absolute WP swing" is almost exactly Mishra's cricket swing, Σ_o p_o·|V(s′_o) − V(s)|. It is also Tango's baseball Leverage Index (LI) before the final step. Both of those sources finish by **dividing by the mean swing, so that an average delivery has LI = 1.0** (Mishra's reference mean is 0.0170 WP per ball) ([arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.14696); [MLB glossary](https://www.mlb.com/glossary/advanced-stats/leverage-index); [Tango archive](https://tangotiger.net/archives/stud0069.shtml)). The capped log transform loses this interpretability (LI 2.0 means twice an average ball) and makes cross-season comparisons depend on where the cap falls. The top-20% rule has a further problem: it forces exactly one ball in five to be a "pressure ball" whether a season was full of close finishes or of blowouts. A fixed LI threshold calibrated once on the training population would let the share of pressure balls vary with how close matches actually were. The stakes multiplier would remain as an analogue of baseball's game-level leverage.

A subtler problem is consistency. The swing |V(s′) − V(s)| is exactly attributable only when V is the martingale value function that generated the outcome probabilities p_o. If calibrated ML WP is used for V and empirical outcome probabilities for p_o, the expected signed drift E[V(s′) − V(s)] is non-zero, and part of the measured "leverage" is model drift. The same mismatch inflates WPA. In Mishra's data, raw batting WPA summed to about +62 wins, which is pure bias, so WPA must be de-drifted by WP bin before roles are compared ([arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.14696)). Pressure Lab should compute swing from one surface throughout, or at minimum report the mean signed drift per state as a diagnostic. Cheap complements include an over-level leverage (expected |dWP| over the next six balls), which is less noisy for bowlers, and the Bhattacharjee–Lemmer Pressure Index (an RRR ratio multiplied by weighted wickets) as a WP-free cross-check for chases ([arXiv 2505.01849](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.01849v2)). That paper's exact original formula was not retrieved.

## Pressure skill is mostly overall skill: clutch barely replicates anywhere

The cross-sport evidence is consistent. Tango's standardised clutch scores for 340 hitters had an SD of 1.14 against 1.0 expected from luck alone. That is "clutch hitting exists, albeit at a very small level", and the most detectable cases were worth about 2 runs a season ([Tango](https://tangotiger.net/clutch.html)). Carleton measured the year-to-year correlation of a WP-based clutch measure at **0.074** ([Baseball Prospectus](https://www.baseballprospectus.com/news/article/24401/baseball-therapy-i-believe-in-clutch-hitting/)). Cramer's best 1969 clutch hitters reverted to normal in 1970 ([SABR](https://sabr.org/journal/article/clutch-hitting-and-the-cramer-test/)). Tennis gives a useful reframing. Klaassen & Magnus found that top players' apparent outperformance on big points came mostly from **weaker players underperforming**, not from champions lifting their level ([ITF Coaching Review](https://itfcoachingreview.com/index.php/journal/article/download/478/1297/1944)). NBA free-throw data show average choking of about 5–10% under pressure, a population effect that is hard to pin on individuals ([TAU PDF](https://www.autolab.sites.tau.ac.il/_files/ugd/f0ac3e_dccc848e01c94efda606f97ade89edb4.pdf)). In cricket, the Delta system's breakout bowlers regressed sharply the following season ([Misra & Rajaraman 2026](https://www.cs.columbia.edu/%7Emisra/delta-2026.pdf)). No published study reports the year-to-year reliability of a T20 pressure delta, so Pressure Lab's own split-half result is the best evidence available.

Baseball's WPA family gives the template for separating opportunity from skill. **WPA** is context-dependent value. **WPA/LI** sums each play's WPA divided by its leverage, giving context-neutral wins. **Clutch = WPA/pLI − WPA/LI** measures how much better a player did in high leverage than his own leverage-neutral rate ([FanGraphs "All About Clutch"](https://blogs.fangraphs.com/all-about-clutch/)). Pressure Lab's WPA on pressure balls corresponds to the first quantity, and its pressure-minus-normal delta to the third. Computing all three explicitly would show users which part of a high rating is opportunity and which is outperformance. ESPNcricinfo's Smart Stats multiply runs by a pressure index ([ESPN explainer](https://www.espn.in/cricket/story/_/id/26312449)), which blends the two, and that is the trap to avoid.

For the delta itself, Tango-style regression applies directly: shrunk = raw × n/(n+k), with k = n(1−R)/R derived from Pressure Lab's own reliability. Reliability measured on half the sample must first be stepped up with Spearman–Brown (2r/(1+r)). Carleton's original stabilisation thresholds were criticised for being off by roughly a factor of two for exactly this reason ([Retrosheet/Pavitt](https://www.retrosheet.org/resources/retrosheet-a-c.pdf)). Odd/even match splits are also noisy for players with 20–70 matches, so averaging over many random half-splits, or using an ICC from a one-way ANOVA, would stabilise the reliability estimate. Showing the shrinkage factor n/(n+k) beside each player tells users how much of a score comes from data and how much from the prior. A large k, likely thousands of balls, means most deltas shrink to near zero. That is the honest result.

## A crossed mixed model should replace the two-stage pipeline

Pressure Lab currently estimates expected runs and wickets with a Poisson GLM, adjusts for opposition through an opponent tier plus team Elo, and then shrinks each player's residual separately. The literature points to fitting all of this jointly. RAPM is the clearest precedent. Sill replaced OLS adjusted plus-minus with ridge regression, judged models on held-out games, and reported nearly double the accuracy ([Sill 2010](https://supermariogiacomazzo.github.io/STOR538_WEBSITE/Articles/Basketball/Basketball_Sill.pdf)). Baseball Prospectus's DRA puts batter, pitcher, catcher and umpire in crossed random effects and later moved from lme4 to fully Bayesian Stan ([BP DRA 2015](https://baseballprospectus.com/?p=29118); [BP 2021](https://www.baseballprospectus.com/news/article/66474/deserved-stats-for-the-2021-season/)). In cricket, an additive batter-minus-bowler rating model already exists ([arXiv 2209.06346](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2209.06346)). The proposed specification is: ball outcome ~ over-level state + target/RRR + venue + competition × season + (1 + pressure | batter) + (1 + pressure | bowler). Opponent quality then comes from the actual bowlers and batters faced, so team Elo becomes redundant for player ratings. The **pressure random slope** is the quantity the noisy delta is trying to estimate, and it is pooled jointly with each player's overall level. RAPM practice suggests that an informative prior equal to the player's all-situations level is the principled form of "shrink the delta toward zero" ([APBRmetrics](https://apbr.org/metrics/viewtopic.php?p=33130)). The cost estimate of minutes in glmmTMB or lme4 on about 250k balls, and less on aggregated cell counts, is the note-writer's estimate and has not been benchmarked. A 2026 comparison warns that Laplace-based packages bias variance components ([arXiv 2606.15933](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.15933)). Those components drive shrinkage, so the final production fit belongs in Stan or PyMC.

The outcome model should also change. Separate Poisson (runs) and logistic (wicket) models ignore the run–wicket trade-off created by aggression, and runs are lumpy at 0, 1, 4 and 6 rather than Poisson-distributed. The academic standard is a single multinomial over {0,1,2,3,4,6,W} with player baselines multiplied by state factors. Davis, Perera & Swartz use a Dirichlet prior on batter baselines equivalent to about **60 balls** ([SFU PDF](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/t20sim.pdf)), and Ganesh shrinks with n/(n+50) ([arXiv 2604.13861](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.13861v2)). Opta's next-ball model likewise outputs the full outcome distribution, using hierarchical Bayesian player and venue ratings plus an in-game "pitch behaving vs expectation" term ([Opta Analyst](https://theanalyst.com/articles/opta-next-ball-predictor)). A LightGBM softmax or a multinomial logit would make xR = Σk·p_k and xW = p_W coherent by construction. The cricWAR author argues for over-level state over phase buckets ([Rafique, CMSAC 2022](https://www.stat.cmu.edu/cmsac/conference/2022/assets/pdf/HassanRafique.pdf)). The notes found no published head-to-head comparison of multinomial against Poisson-plus-logistic in cricket, so Pressure Lab would have to run that test itself.

Three components can be borrowed directly. Bandyopadhyay prices a dismissal through a Bellman value function and **centres the realised wicket cost on its expectation (xDAR)**. The wicket charge is then a mean-zero residual, and a player pays only for dismissal behaviour above the league hazard ([arXiv 2608.18020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.18020)). Delta's iterative opposition fixpoint over the batter–bowler graph converges in about 8 passes and "materially reorders" high-leverage bowlers. It is a cheap interim step if the full mixed model is delayed ([Misra & Rajaraman 2026](https://www.cs.columbia.edu/%7Emisra/delta-2026.pdf)). Delta also warns that a par table not conditioned on the target is "conceptually wrong for a chase", so Pressure Lab's expected-runs baseline for chase pressure balls must be conditioned on the target. For weakness heatmaps, cells with fewer than 20 balls are noise. The fix is Gamma–Poisson shrinkage, (R + k)/(E + k) with k equal to about 50–60 balls of expected runs, toward the batter's *own* overall ratio rather than toward 1, with cells greyed out when their interval spans 1. Low-rank batter × bowler factorisation is a later option that nobody has validated in cricket ([arXiv 2010.15607](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2010.15607)). For the all-rounder rating, Delta, Bandyopadhyay and Smart Stats all add batting and bowling in one shared currency per ball ([Delta](https://www.cs.columbia.edu/%7Emisra/delta-2026.pdf); [ESPN](https://www.espn.in/cricket/story/_/id/26312449)). Pressure Lab should therefore **sum un-standardised pressure WPA (or net runs) per match before standardising**. Standardising each discipline first over-rewards part-time bowlers and discards volume.

For uncertainty, a joint posterior gives coherent rank distributions. Gelman, Hill & Yajima show that partial pooling handles top-10 multiplicity without Bonferroni widening ([arXiv 0907.2478](https://arxiv.org/abs/0907.2478)). Shen & Louis show that ranking posterior means ranks players poorly and offer the triple-goal GR estimator, implemented as `HETOP::triple_goal` ([RePEc](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:jorssb:v:60:y:1998:i:2:p:455-471); [HETOP](https://search.r-project.org/CRAN/refmans/HETOP/html/triple_goal.html)). Pressure Lab's match bootstrap with P(top 10) is ahead of most cricket systems. Its percentile intervals for shrunk estimates are likely too narrow at small n, though, and they omit uncertainty from the expected-runs and expected-wickets GLMs unless those GLMs are refit in every replicate.

## A falsifiable validation plan

Validation should follow the Efron–Morris and Sill template: fit on period t and predict period t+1 ([Efron & Morris 1975](https://www.medicine.mcgill.ca/epidemiology/hanley/bios602/MultilevelData/EfronMorrisJASA1975.pdf)). For WP, use rolling-origin splits (train up to 2022 and test on 2023, then up to 2023 and test on 2024, and so on), keeping 2026 as an untouched test year. Score each split by innings and balls-left band. Add a Brier decomposition that separates reliability, where drift shows up, from resolution. If drift grows with distance from the training era, that justifies the scoring-environment covariate. For ball outcomes, fit on 2022–24 and test on 2025. Compare four models on held-out *pressure* balls using multiclass log loss and per-class reliability: context only, the current pipeline, the GLMM with pressure slopes, and the GLMM with recency. For player ratings, use balls-faced-weighted Spearman correlation and RMSE between period-t ratings and period-(t+1) pressure runs above expected, and check that 90% intervals cover next-period values about 90% of the time. Tune the decay constant, the eligibility thresholds and the composite weights only on the validation period. Learn the composite weights by ridge-regressing future outcomes on current components, not by multiplying prior weights by reliability.

The decisive test is whether **the pressure slope improves held-out log loss on pressure balls beyond each player's overall level**. If it does not, the product's core claim, that pressure skill is a distinct trait, is unsupported. The rating should then be reframed as "overall skill weighted by leverage" (Mishra also warns that good per-ball calibration does not guarantee calibrated aggregate WP or pressure metrics, so aggregates need their own checks ([arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.14696))). A cheap side test of a related hypothesis: add "dots in the last k balls" and "balls since the last boundary" to the wicket model, controlling for state and bowler. That checks the widely asserted dot-ball-pressure-causes-wickets story, for which the notes found no rigorous T20 evidence ([The Roar](https://www.theroar.com.au/?p=234334); [PLOS ONE](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0287700)).

## Source reliability flags

Several load-bearing sources are unreviewed, so the recommendations built on them deserve proportionate caution. **Mishra (arXiv 2608.14696, 2026)** is a single-author preprint that has not been peer reviewed, yet it underpins the DP diagnosis, the two-surface design and the leverage normalisation. Its numbers should be replicated on Pressure Lab's own data before any rebuild. **Bandyopadhyay (arXiv 2608.18020)** is a preprint, and only its abstract was read; it reports no validation and no code. **Ganesh (arXiv 2604.13861)** has been submitted to JQAS but is not yet accepted. **Delta (Misra & Rajaraman 2026)** is a technical report rather than a journal paper. The Bhattacharjee–Lemmer formula was taken from a later restatement. The FanGraphs LI bands (below 0.85 low, above 2.0 high) were recalled from memory and are unverified, so they are left out above. The FanGraphs and Carleton pages returned 403 errors and were cited from search snippets. Tango's page gives two internally inconsistent regression formulas. The NFL clutch figure (0.1% of variance explained, 35 QBs) and the NBA R² of 0.31 come from blogs. CricViz and Smart Stats methodologies are proprietary. The 2026 IPL scoring figures came from a mirror domain and were not cited. All compute-time estimates are unbenchmarked inferences. No source anywhere has validated a T20 pressure or clutch rating out of sample.

## Conclusion

The research turns Pressure Lab's apparent weaknesses into design constraints. Low delta reliability, first-innings drift and DP miscalibration are what the literature predicts for any system that measures clutch from outcome residuals and uses an independence-assuming end-game. They are not implementation bugs. The deeper implication is that "pressure rating" and "WP display" pull in opposite statistical directions: attribution wants an internally consistent martingale, while display wants calibration. A product that names that trade-off openly, publishes an error bound and lets the data decide, through the pressure-slope test, whether pressure skill exists is more defensible than any system now public.

Pressure Lab can then make a claim no cricket rating currently makes: that its pressure scores were checked out of time and either earned their place or were honestly reframed. The quick wins need no new modelling stack. Doing them first will also show whether the larger mixed-model rebuild is worth its cost.
